Bulgarian solitaire is a mathematical game played with cards. One starts with n cards divided into stacks and placed face down. One step of the game consists of taking one card from every stack to form a new stack. Figure 1 shows one move in a game with 5 cards, from the configuration with stack sizes (3,1,1) to the configuration (3,2). Since the cards are treated as indistinguishable, a configuration is encoded by its stack sizes. These stack sizes are traditionally ordered in weakly decreasing order and represented as a partition of n. Thus, Bulgarian solitaire defines a discrete dynamical system on the finite set 𝒫n of partitions of n, and every sequence of iterates is eventually periodic.

According to the survey [16], which also discusses the origin of the game’s name, Bulgarian solitaire initially spread among mathematicians through informal conversations. A central observation was that, when the number of cards is triangular, n = k(k + 1)/2, every initial configuration eventually settles into the partition (k, k − 1, …, 1). This observation inspired several mathematicians to seek a proof (see, e.g., [2, 6]) and to investigate the behavior for general n. Early analyses of the general case appeared in [3, 25]; see also [1, 14]. Gardner popularized the game in his Scientific American column, bringing it to an international audience [11].

The dynamics of Bulgarian solitaire have been studied from several perspectives, including the enumeration of periodic orbits and the study of periodicity [3, 12, 14, 20], as well as the analysis of Garden of Eden partitions [18, 19]. Related work examines the game graph and the distribution of level sizes [7, 13, 23]. Beyond the classical game, numerous variants have been studied (see, e.g., [4, 5, 9, 15, 17, 21, 22, 26]), including probabilistic versions (see, e.g., [8, 24]).

In the classical game, the cards are treated as indistinguishable and only the stack sizes are tracked. This paper asks what happens to individual cards as the game proceeds. A return of the stack sizes need not return each card to its original position, so the labeled configurations can have longer periods than their underlying partitions. To study this behavior, we extend Bulgarian solitaire to partitions with distinguishable cells, denoted by 𝒫n⋆, and define four natural actions T1, T2, T3, T4. These actions differ in whether a card is removed from the top or the bottom of each stack and in the order in which the removed cards are arranged to form a new stack. We determine the sizes of the periodic orbits for all four actions. The main work lies in the analysis of T2, for which we develop a coloring algorithm on Ferrers diagrams that describes how the cards are permuted over one period of the underlying partition.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews partitions, Ferrers diagrams, and classical Bulgarian solitaire, including the characterization of periodic points. Section 3 introduces the four actions, establishes their relationship with the classical game, and uses reversal to reduce the orbit-size analysis to T1 and T2. Section 4 determines the periodic orbit sizes under T1 and develops the coloring algorithm for T2. Section 5 completes the analysis by determining the exact periodic orbit sizes under T2. Finally, Section 6 studies the interaction with conjugation and the resulting orbit-reversing symmetries.
