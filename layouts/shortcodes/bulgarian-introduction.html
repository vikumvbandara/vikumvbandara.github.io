Bulgarian solitaire is a mathematical game played with cards. One starts with n cards divided into stacks and placed face down. One step of the game consists of taking one card from every stack to form a new stack. The game is won when the sequence of arrangements becomes periodic. Figure 1 shows one move in a game with 5 cards, from the configuration with stack sizes (3,1,1) to the configuration (3,2). Because the cards are indistinguishable, the configuration is encoded only by the stack sizes. These stack sizes are traditionally ordered in weakly decreasing order and represented as a partition of n. Thus, Bulgarian solitaire defines a discrete dynamical system on the set 𝒫n of partitions of n.

The game was introduced by Karatsuba during a visit to the Bulgarian Academy of Sciences in May 1980 [7]. The following year, Toom provided the first proof, demonstrating that for certain numbers of cards, the game inevitably settles into a “triangular configuration” [28]. Bojanov and Eriksson published treatments that same year [3, 8]. Gardner popularized the game in his Scientific American column, bringing it to an international audience [14]. Akin and Davis discussed the game in The American Mathematical Monthly [1], and Hopkins reviewed its 30 years of history in [19].

Subsequent work examined the cycles and dynamics of Bulgarian solitaire. Brandt studied the cycles formed by the stacks in Bulgarian solitaire and found the number of distinct cycles (orbits) for partitions of n [5]. Later work explored cycle counts and periodicity [15, 17, 21]. Other studies analyzed game-tree level sizes and depth generating functions [10, 16], as well as the limiting behavior of growing orbits [26]. Related work on partitions considers column-to-row operations [18], an inductive proof of a result concerning Bulgarian solitaire [23], and connections of the game to Young tableaux [13] and to Owari marching groups [4].

In addition to the studies of the classical game, there are many variants of Bulgarian solitaire, including Austrian and Montreal solitaire, multiplayer forms and proposed variants [2, 6, 9, 20, 25, 29]. More recently, Meyerowitz et al. introduced Floridian solitaire [22]. Probabilistic studies examine random Bulgarian solitaire, its random q-proportion variant, and the limit shape of stochastic Bulgarian solitaire [11, 12, 27]. The surveys [7, 24] provide broader reviews of the mathematics of the game and its variants.

In the classical game, the cards are indistinguishable and only the stack sizes are tracked. This paper asks what happens to individual cards as the game proceeds. For this purpose, we extend the Bulgarian solitaire to partitions with distinguishable cells, denoted by 𝒫n⋆, and define four natural actions T1, T2, T3, T4. The actions differ in whether cards are removed from the top or the bottom of the stack and in the order in which the removed cards are ordered to form a new stack. Our main results characterize the orbit sizes of these actions. In particular, we show that a configuration is periodic under any of the four actions if and only if its underlying partition is periodic under classical Bulgarian solitaire, and we determine the exact sizes of the resulting orbits.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the necessary definitions of partitions, Ferrers diagrams, and classical Bulgarian solitaire, including the characterization of periodic points. In Section 3, we introduce the four natural actions on partitions with distinguishable cells, and we show how they are related to the classical Bulgarian solitaire and to one another through reversal. This reduces our work to analyzing the orbits of T1 and T2 only. In Section 4, we begin the orbit size analysis: we determine the orbit sizes for T1 and we develop a coloring algorithm to analyze T2. In Section 5, we use that analysis to determine the exact orbit sizes under T2. Finally, in Section 6, we show how conjugation interacts with our actions and how it reverses the direction of periodic orbits.
